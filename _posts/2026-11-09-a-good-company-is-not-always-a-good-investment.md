I've been thinking about how the same company can be a great investment for one person and a terrible investment for another.

Imagine an angel investing $100,000 in a startup at a $5 million valuation. The company eventually sells for $100 million. Ignoring dilution, the angel makes 20 times their money.

Now imagine a billion-dollar venture fund investing $10 million in the same company at a $50 million valuation. At the same exit price, the fund doubles its money. A perfectly respectable return, but it contributes just 1% of the fund's capital in profit.

The angel might remember this investment for the rest of their life. The fund might barely remember it.

Both invested in a company that succeeded. Yet only one got an outcome that really mattered.

But I wonder if those expectations eventually change the company itself.

Once a company raises venture capital, a perfectly reasonable outcome might no longer be enough. The company may need to grow much faster, enter markets it otherwise wouldn't, or keep raising money to pursue an outcome large enough to justify the investment.

A business that might have survived for decades could end up failing while trying to become something much bigger.

Of course, without that capital, some companies would never get built in the first place.

What I find interesting is that funding isn't just a way to finance a company's ambitions. It can quietly determine what those ambitions have to be.

And perhaps, in some cases, the decision about how big a company needs to become is made long before anyone knows how big it could become.
